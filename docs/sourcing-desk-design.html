01The job we are automating

An engineer is handed a requirement — sometimes a manufacturer part number, sometimes a description of what the thing must do. He has to come back with a price, a lead time, and a recommendation he can defend.

What that costs him today, per part:

  • Work out what the part actually is and pull its real specifications out of a datasheet PDF.
  • Search — in two languages, across marketplaces, manufacturer sites and distributors — for anyone who sells it.
  • Open thirty tabs. Read prices that are quoted at different quantities, in different currencies, under different incoterms.
  • Convert, reconcile, add freight and duty and VAT, and discover the cheapest quote was not the cheapest option.
  • Email the suppliers who did not publish a price at all, then wait and chase.
  • Do it again next month, having forgotten what he paid last time and which supplier delivered late.

Hours per part, and almost none of it is judgement. It is the kind of work that quietly gets skipped under deadline, which is how a company ends up paying well over the market rate for a sensor.

02What it does

One sentence: it researches a part across the web and returns a decision-ready comparison table.

The system is a research agent, not a chatbot and not a mail client. Its output is a structured table of real options — who sells it, at what price, at what quantity, landed in Tashkent, arriving when, matching which specifications, from a supplier we have or have not dealt with before. Then it answers follow-up questions about that table, and asks its own when the request is ambiguous.

Core loop

Research & compare

Search wide, extract real supplier data, normalise it, cost it, rank it. This is the product and everything else serves it.

Supporting

Remember

He tells it what he bought and how it went, in one sentence. That history sharpens every future comparison.

Fallback

Ask suppliers

When the web has no usable price, draft an RFQ email. A tool the agent reaches for — not the centre of the system.

Design correction from v0.1

The first draft built the whole system around the RFQ email loop. That was the most novel piece, not the most useful one. Web research is where the time actually goes, so web research is the centre. Email is a fallback for the minority of suppliers who publish nothing.

03System overview

Four layers. A sourcing request is a persistent object rather than a chat turn — it can sit half-finished while a supplier is chased, so the graph is checkpointed.

04Search strategy

Search everywhere. Weight what he trusts. Both halves matter — scoping search to a fixed list of favourites means never discovering a better supplier, and searching wide without weighting drowns real suppliers in marketing pages.

Two engines, because they see different webs

Google indexes the English-language supplier web. Baidu indexes the Chinese industrial web — manufacturers' own sites, Made-in-China listings, regional trading companies — much of which Google systematically under-surfaces. For a buyer sourcing Chinese sensors, running only Google means missing the actual manufacturer and buying from a reseller two steps down the chain.

So every part query runs against both, plus direct site search on the marketplaces themselves. Queries are issued in English and, for Baidu, translated — the part number is usually the strongest search key and travels across both.

Preference is a weight, not a filter

# every candidate result is scored, none are excluded
score = relevance * source_weight * freshness

source_weight:
  favourite marketplace   1.5    # his usual haunts, ranked up
  known manufacturer      1.4    # buying at the source
  bought from before      1.3    # proven, from the purchase log
  unknown                 1.0    # still shown, just lower
  known bad / reseller    0.4    # demoted, not hidden

A source that keeps producing good results climbs. A new supplier that turns out well gets promoted to a favourite the first time he logs a purchase from them — the registry learns instead of being maintained by hand.

Seeded with what he already uses, and it grows from there:

No entry is hand-maintained after seeding. A supplier he logs a purchase from is promoted automatically; a source that repeatedly yields nothing usable drifts down. He can add a local distributor or a new marketplace at any time and it takes effect on the next search.

05Getting the data

Discovery finds pages. Extraction turns them into offers. This is the part that decides whether the product works, so it is built as a ladder — each rung is tried, and failure falls through to the next.

What each rung actually costs us

TargetMethodHonest assessment
Manufacturer & distributor sitesfetch + LLM extractEasy and reliable. Most of our good data comes from here.
Made-in-Chinamobile JSON endpointStructured data without HTML parsing. Best value per hour spent.
Alibaba.comPlaywright + stealthModerate anti-bot. Works with fingerprint patches and pacing. Expect to maintain it.
Search resultsSerpApiGoogle and Baidu through one API, 250 searches/month free. Brave API as a backup index.

Chinese pages are translated, not skipped

Baidu returns Chinese-language results, and the good ones — manufacturers' own product pages, trading company listings — are entirely in Chinese. We follow them and extract from them directly rather than only chasing results that happen to have an English version.

Mechanically this costs almost nothing: the extraction model reads Chinese natively, so it pulls price, MOQ, lead time and specifications straight from the Chinese page and emits an English structured offer. What it does add is a display obligation — every row keeps its original page title and URL alongside the translated fields, so he can open the source and see what the agent actually read.

Watch for this

Chinese listings quote prices in CNY and quantities in Chinese units. Currency and unit conversion happen at extraction time, not display time, and the original values stay in the record so an error is traceable rather than baked in.

Be honest about this

Scrapers break. Sites change markup, add challenges, rotate endpoints. The design assumption is not that extraction always succeeds — it is that failure falls through cleanly to the next rung and finally to a human paste or an RFQ email, and that the table says which rung each row came from.

Four things this table does that a spreadsheet of scraped prices does not:

  • Every row states its provenance. A verified quote and a listing scraped from a marketplace are not the same evidence, and the table never pretends they are.
  • The cheapest unit price is not the cheapest row. At $38.50 EXW with MOQ 50, the Shenzhen listing lands above the manufacturer's $41 FOB. That inversion is the whole point.
  • Disqualification is visible, not silent. The cheapest option on the page fails on output signal — 4–20 mA where the requirement is RS485 — so it is shown, struck from ranking, with the failing parameter named.
  • Missing data is a state, not a blank. A supplier with no published price is marked rfq sent, not dropped.

Quantity sensitivity sits underneath it: change 50 to 10 and the ranking reshuffles, because MOQ and tier breaks bite. Since his volumes vary, that recomputation is a first-class feature rather than a footnote.

07Landed cost

The number that decides things, and the number nobody computes. Uzbekistan applies VAT at 12 % on CIF plus duty, with duty typically around 20 % for electronics and ranging 5–70 % by HS code. Below $100 is de minimis.

# computed in Python, never generated as text by the model
CIF      = goods + freight + insurance
duty     = CIF * duty_rate(hs_code)
vat      = (CIF + duty) * 0.12
total    = CIF + duty + vat + bank_fee + broker_fee
per_unit = total / qty

Twenty units quoted at $45 FOB:

LineBasisUSD
Goods20 × $45900.00
Freight to Tashkentquoted120.00
CIF1 020.00
Customs duty20 % of CIF204.00
VAT12 % of CIF + duty146.88
Total landed$68.55 per unit1 370.88

A $45 sticker price is a $68.55 part — 52 % higher. Set that against a $52 DDP quote where freight and duty are already inside the number, and the ranking inverts. This is arithmetic a spreadsheet could do, if anyone ever did it.

Freight, with no usual forwarder

There is no regular forwarder to model, so freight is a generic estimate with editable defaults rather than a lookup. One domain fact shapes the whole table: Uzbekistan is landlocked. There is no sea freight option — everything from China arrives by air, rail or road.

ModeRateTransitFits
Air express (DHL, FedEx)$8–12 / kg5–9 dSmall, urgent, samples
Air freight$4–6 / kg10–15 dMedium batches
Road via Kazakhstan$2–3 / kg15–25 dMid volume, no rush
Rail, China–Central Asia$1.5–2.5 / kg20–30 dBulk, planned ahead

Weight comes from the datasheet where it is stated and from an estimate where it is not — flagged as an estimate either way. Every costing prints the mode and rate it assumed, so a wrong assumption is visible on the row rather than hidden in a total.

Treat these as placeholders

Those rates are starting defaults, not quotes. The first real invoice he logs should replace them. Until then the freight figure is the softest number in the table and the table should say so.

Human gate

Duty follows from HS code, and classification is genuinely ambiguous for technical goods. The agent proposes a code with the tariff line quoted; he overrides it. The assumed code prints on every costing, so a wrong one is visible rather than buried.

08Equivalence checking

“Is this cheaper one actually a substitute?” — the question with the most money attached and the highest chance of an expensive wrong answer. Searching wide guarantees it comes up constantly, because Baidu will surface a dozen near-identical-looking sensors at half the price.

Hard blocker

Disqualifies outright

Output signal RS485 against 4–20 mA. Supply voltage. Probe material. IP rating below requirement. Thread or mounting standard. No price advantage survives these.

Soft difference

A trade-off to weigh

Accuracy ±3 % against ±2 %. Response time. Cable length. Warranty months. Range wider than needed. Scored, not rejected.

Unknown

Say so

The datasheet does not state it. Reported as a gap with a suggested question for the supplier — never quietly assumed to match.

This is also the honest justification for the multimodal requirement in the course brief. Datasheet specifications live in tables and diagrams, frequently as images inside the PDF. Captioning them with a vision model so they become searchable is not a gimmick here — without it, half the specifications are invisible to retrieval.

Critic rule

A part failing any mandatory specification is never ranked and never recommended. It appears as disqualified, with the failing parameter named, so he can overrule it if he knows something the datasheet does not.

09Asking follow-up questions

Half the requests that reach a sourcing engineer are underspecified. Guessing produces a confident, useless table. So the graph has a clarify node that can interrupt from any stage, ask, and resume.

Him

find me FST100-2006A

Desk

Soil temperature / moisture / EC sensor, RS485, Hunan Firstrate. Two things before I search — how many, and is RS485 fixed or would 4–20 mA also work? That second one roughly doubles the supplier pool.

Him

50, RS485 only

Desk

Searching. I’ll assume delivery to Tashkent and quote landed unless you say otherwise.

The rule that keeps it usable

Ask only what changes the answer. Quantity always does — it moves MOQ, tier pricing and freight mode. Whether a spec is mandatory or preferred does, because it decides the size of the candidate pool. The PO number does not. Warranty preference usually does not.

Everything else gets a stated assumption rather than a question. Two questions maximum before doing useful work; anything further can be asked once there is a table to point at.

Mechanically this is the same LangGraph interrupt as the approval gates — the graph pauses, persists, and resumes on his reply. It is not a separate conversation system.

10Purchase log

He tells it what he bought in one sentence, whenever he remembers. The agent then completes the record — because a purchase is written once and cannot be reconstructed later.

Him

bought 56 FST100 from Firstrate, $41 each, came in 18 days

Desk

Got most of it. Filling the gaps:

part FST100-2006A or -2006C?
price basis was $41 FOB, EXW or delivered?
freight + duty what did you pay, if you know
ordered on roughly when?
condition all arrived working?

Him

2006A, FOB, freight was about 90, duty and vat maybe 150, ordered start of june, all fine

Desk

Saved. 56 × FST100-2006A, Hunan Firstrate, $41.00 FOB, $2 296 goods + $90 freight + $150 duties = $45.29 landed per unit. Ordered 1 Jun, received 19 Jun, 18 days actual against no promise recorded.

Firstrate is now a known-good supplier and ranks higher in future searches. Your real air-freight rate works out around $1.60/kg — I’ll use that instead of the default estimate.

Why this asks more than §09 does

The clarify node before a search asks the minimum, because he wants results fast and a wrong assumption costs one re-run.

The purchase log asks for everything, because this record is permanent and unrecoverable. A missing price basis silently corrupts every future comparison against it. The questions are batched into one message rather than asked one at a time, so it still costs him a single reply.

The full record

FieldSourceWhy it earns a question

Everything derived — landed per unit, actual lead days, implied freight rate per kg — is computed and stored alongside, so the History agent can query it without recalculating.

The sophisticated version of this feature is an ERP integration. It would be more impressive and it would never get used. A sentence typed into a chat box is the version that actually accumulates data — and the data is what makes everything else smart:

  • Real prices to compare new quotes against, rather than list prices.
  • Real lead times, which are usually longer than the promised ones.
  • Trust scores that come from delivery record instead of guesswork.
  • Search weighting — a supplier he has bought from is automatically promoted in future ranking.

The log is also what makes the text-to-SQL agent worth having. Without it there is nothing to query and F5 is a toy; with it, “what have we paid for RS485 soil sensors over the last year, and who delivered on time?” is a real question with a real answer.

11RFQ email — the fallback

Some suppliers publish nothing. The listing says contact supplier, or the price shown is at a quantity that does not apply. For those rows the agent drafts an RFQ email rather than leaving a blank cell.

The loop, kept deliberately small:

  1. Draft one email per supplier from the requirement — part, quantity, specs, destination, asking for FOB and DDP, MOQ, lead time, tier breaks and shipping weight.
  2. He approves, edits or drops each one. Nothing sends without that.
  3. Send with a reference code in the subject so replies match back to the request and supplier.
  4. Parse replies — body text, PDF or spreadsheet attachment — into the same offer schema everything else uses, with per-field confidence.
  5. After a few days of silence, draft a chase. Also approved before sending.

Parsed offers land in the same table as scraped ones, marked verified because a quote addressed to us at our quantity is better evidence than a public listing.

This is also where weight comes from

The §07 freight model needs a shipping weight per part and datasheets routinely omit it. Rather than guessing from dimensions, the RFQ simply asks — suppliers know it, it costs nothing to include in a question we are already sending, and the answer is authoritative.

Until a supplier answers, weight is an estimate and the row says so. Once one does, it is stored against the part and reused for every future costing.

Non-negotiable

Nothing is ever sent without approval — not the first RFQ, not a chase. These go to real suppliers under his name, and his relationships are the asset. There is no code path that sends autonomously.

Scope discipline

This section is a fallback and should stay one. If it starts growing an inbox UI, threading rules and templates, the project has drifted back into being a mail client. The table is the product.

12Data model

Deliberately flat, with obvious column names. Vague schemas are exactly where text-to-SQL agents fail — they produce syntactically valid, semantically wrong queries that look plausible.

parts(id, mpn, manufacturer, description, category,
      hs_code, datasheet_url, lifecycle)

part_specs(id, part_id, key, value, unit, mandatory)   -- flexible KV: handles "anything technical"

sources(id, name, domain, kind, access, region, currency,
      ships_to, weight, typical_lead_days, times_used)  -- weight, not a filter (§04)

suppliers(id, name, source_id, country, contact_email,
      trust_score, times_used, on_time_rate, notes)

requests(id, title, part_id, qty, qty_tiers, need_by,
      destination, incoterm_pref, status, created)

offers(id, request_id, supplier_id, part_id, qty, unit_price,
      currency, incoterm, moq, tier_breaks, lead_days,
      warranty_months, url, source_kind, rung, confidence,
      state, captured_at)                               -- rung: which extraction tier produced it

landed_costs(id, offer_id, qty, goods, freight, hs_code,
      duty_pct, duty, vat, fees, total, per_unit, computed_at)

purchases(id, part_id, supplier_id, qty, unit_price,
      currency, price_basis, lead_days_actual,
      purchased_on, notes)                              -- filled from one sentence (§10)

rfqs(id, request_id, supplier_id, ref_code, thread_id,
      sent_at, status, last_chase_at)

Three columns doing unusual work:

  • part_specs as key/value rather than fixed columns is what lets the system handle a soil sensor this week and a pressure transmitter next week without a migration.
  • offers.rung and confidence record how the data was obtained. The critic reads them, and the table displays them — a price scraped from a listing four months ago is not treated as equal to a quote received on Tuesday.
  • purchases.price_basis exists because of the §10 dialogue. Logging $41 without knowing whether it included freight would silently corrupt every future comparison.

Why flash-lite is the right default, and where it is not

Most of this pipeline is high-volume, structurally simple work — read a listing, pull out price and MOQ and lead time, emit JSON to a fixed schema. That is precisely what a lightweight model is built for, and doing it on a large model would be paying reasoning prices for extraction work.

Two jobs are different in kind: supervisor routing and equivalence checking. Deciding whether a 4–20 mA sensor can substitute for an RS485 one is real reasoning with a real cost to getting it wrong.

The advantage of going through OpenRouter

Model choice becomes a config line rather than an architecture decision. Run everything on flash-lite, measure with the §14 golden set, and if equivalence or routing accuracy is weak, point only those two nodes at a stronger model. Per-task routing costs one dictionary.

Where it runs

Playwright is the only component with real infrastructure demands, and it sets the floor. Headless Chromium wants 2 GB; it survives on 1 GB with --disable-dev-shm-usage, --ipc=host and one browser at a time, but fragilely.

OptionGivesVerdict
Local machineEverything, no limitsDevelopment — P0 through P3 run here
e2-small, europe-central22 GB, ~60 ms from Tashkent, on the $300 creditMVP deployment — the recommendation
e2-micro, us-central11 GB, Always Free forever, ~200 msFallback when credit runs out

The decision that actually matters

Not which host — keep the scraper behind an interface from the first commit, so it can run in-process locally and as a remote job in production. That is a few lines now and a rewrite later.

Everything else — FastAPI, SQLite, embedded Qdrant, FastEmbed — is light enough that hosting is not a design constraint.

Embeddings stay local, deliberately

OpenRouter does expose an OpenAI-compatible /embeddings endpoint, so one key genuinely could cover everything. FastEmbed is still the better call here: the datasheet corpus is small, embedding runs happen in bursts during ingestion, and doing it locally removes a paid call, a network dependency and a rate limit from the hottest loop in the system. If the corpus ever outgrows the laptop, switching is a one-line change.

Budgets to respect

SerpApi is the binding constraint — 250 searches a month, and a single thorough sourcing run can burn five. Cache by part number, never re-search on an incidental chat message.

Model spend is the looser one: flash-lite sits in the cheapest tier and a full run should cost cents, but scraped pages are long. Truncate aggressively before sending a page to extraction rather than posting whole documents into a 1M-token window because it fits.

Two things worth saying in the defence

F13 is unusually good here. Watching a table populate row by row as the agent searches Baidu, opens a listing and extracts a price is a far better demo than tokens streaming into a chat bubble.

F11 has real ground truth. We hand-compute the landed costs and hand-check the spec matches for a fixed set of parts, so evaluation measures correctness rather than plausibility. Most projects cannot do this.

15Build order

MVP boundary

One part number in. Search runs on Google and Baidu. At least five real offers extracted from live sites. Landed cost computed. One table out, with provenance per row. Nothing else until that runs end to end.

P0

Foundation

State schema, config, SQLite created, source registry seeded with his real favourites and starting weights. Datasheet ingestion into Qdrant with image captioning.

P1

Scout — the spine

Search across both engines, weighted ranking, then the extraction ladder rung by rung: direct fetch first, Made-in-China JSON second, Playwright last. Built and tested before anything else, because every other component consumes its output.

P2

Analyst and the table

Landed cost calculator unit-tested against hand-computed figures first — it is arithmetic and it must be exactly right. Then normalisation, the comparison table, and quantity sensitivity.

P3

Memory and clarify

Purchase log with the follow-up dialogue, History agent over it, and the clarify interrupt. Small features, disproportionate effect on how the thing feels to use.

P4

Orchestration and critic

Supervisor, critic, graph wiring with step budgets. Late on purpose — there is nothing worth routing between until the specialists work.

P5

Equivalence, RFQ, ship

Alternate-part checking, the RFQ email fallback, golden evaluation set, Langfuse, frontend, deploy.

Equivalence checking (§08) is the most impressive feature and it is still scheduled fifth, because a table of real offers with correct landed cost is worth more than a clever feature sitting on top of nothing.

Still open

  1. How wide is a search. Twenty candidate pages costs more SerpApi calls and more extraction than five, and finds better options. What is the right default before the 250/month cap bites? Answer this once there is real behaviour to measure.

Both are tuning decisions that need data we do not have yet. Neither blocks the build.
